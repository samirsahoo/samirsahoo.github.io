SRE Fundamentals: SLOs, Error Budgets and Incident Management

Site reliability engineering explained: SLIs, SLOs and SLAs, choosing good SLIs, error budgets and burn-rate alerting, toil and automation, on-call, incident management roles and lifecycle, blameless postmortems, and SRE in a platform organisation.

Simple definition#

Site reliability engineering (SRE) applies software engineering to operations. Its central idea: decide how reliable a service needs to be (a Service Level Objective), measure it, and use the gap between 100% and the objective — the error budget — to balance reliability work against feature delivery.

SLI, SLO, SLA#

Term Meaning Example
SLI — indicator A measurement of user-visible behaviour % of /checkout requests that succeed within 300 ms
SLO — objective Target for an SLI over a window 99.9% over 28 days
SLA — agreement A contract with consequences 99.5% monthly, or service credits

Set SLOs stricter than SLAs so you act before breaching contracts. Not every service needs an SLA; every important service needs SLOs.

Good SLIs#

Service type SLIs
Request/response (APIs, web) Availability (good / valid requests), latency (requests faster than threshold)
Data pipelines Freshness, completeness, correctness
Storage Durability, availability of reads/writes

Measure as close to the user as possible (load balancer or gateway, synthetic checks, real-user monitoring), as a ratio of good events / valid events.

Error budgets#

Error budget = 1 − SLO. A 99.9% SLO over 30 days allows about 43 minutes of full unavailability (or the equivalent in failed requests).

SLO Allowed downtime per 30 days
99% ~7.2 hours
99.5% ~3.6 hours
99.9% ~43 minutes
99.95% ~22 minutes
99.99% ~4.3 minutes

Error budget policy (agreed with product owners): while budget remains, ship features; when it is exhausted, prioritise reliability work and slow risky releases.

Burn-rate alerting#

Alert when the budget is being consumed too fast, not on every error spike:

Alert Condition (for a 99.9% SLO) Meaning
Page (fast burn) Burn rate ≥ 14.4 over 1 h and 5 min 2% of the monthly budget gone in an hour
Page (medium) Burn rate ≥ 6 over 6 h and 30 min 5% in six hours
Ticket (slow burn) Burn rate ≥ 1 over 3 days On track to exhaust the budget

Multi-window conditions reduce false alarms and still detect fast incidents. Tools such as Sloth, Pyrra or OpenSLO definitions generate these rules for Prometheus.

Toil and automation#

Toil is manual, repetitive, automatable work that scales with load and has no lasting value (restarting pods, manual certificate renewals, provisioning by ticket). SRE teams cap toil (Google’s guideline: under 50% of time) and automate it away — often by building it into the platform.

Incident management#

Roles#

Role Responsibility
Incident commander Owns coordination and decisions; does not debug
Operations / technical lead Investigates and mitigates
Communications lead Status page, stakeholders, executives
Scribe Timeline of events and decisions

Principles#

  • Mitigate first (roll back, fail over, scale, disable a feature flag), find root cause later.
  • Declare incidents early; it is cheap to downgrade.
  • Use severity levels with clear criteria (user impact, revenue, data, security).
  • Communicate on a cadence, even when there is no news.

Blameless postmortems#

A postmortem records impact, timeline, contributing factors, what went well, and owned, prioritised actions. “Blameless” means focusing on systems and decisions given the information available — people should feel safe to share details, or you will never learn the real causes.

On-call that is sustainable#

  • Alerts must be actionable and tied to SLOs; delete noisy alerts.
  • Runbooks linked from every alert.
  • Rotations of 5–8 people; follow-the-sun where possible; compensation and recovery time.
  • Track pages per shift; > 2 incidents per shift is a reliability signal, not a staffing one.

SRE in a platform organisation#

Platform teams bake SRE practices into golden paths: default SLOs and dashboards per service template, burn-rate alerts, runbook templates and incident tooling.

Common mistakes#

Common mistake100% as the target

Unachievable and paralysing: every change becomes risky. Users cannot tell 99.99% from 100% through their own networks.

Common mistakeAlerting on causes (CPU 80%)

Page on symptoms users feel (SLO burn); use causes for dashboards and diagnosis.

Common mistakePostmortems without follow-through

Actions without owners or deadlines repeat the incident. Track them like product work.

Quick check#

What is the difference between an SLI, an SLO and an SLA? An SLI measures user-visible behaviour; an SLO is the target for it; an SLA is a contractual commitment with consequences, set looser than the SLO.

How much downtime does a 99.9% SLO allow per 30 days? About 43 minutes.

What is an error budget policy? An agreement on what happens as budget is consumed — ship freely while budget remains; prioritise reliability and slow risky changes when it is exhausted.

Why alert on burn rate instead of error rate? Burn rate relates errors to the SLO, alerting only when the budget is threatened; multi-window conditions catch fast and slow burns with fewer false alarms.

What does the incident commander do? Coordinates the response, assigns roles, makes decisions and manages communication — without doing the hands-on debugging.

What is toil? Manual, repetitive, automatable work that scales with load and has no lasting value.

Summary#

  • Define SLIs close to users; set SLOs; derive error budgets and policies.
  • Alert on multi-window burn rates; keep alerts actionable.
  • Run incidents with clear roles; mitigate first; blameless postmortems with owned actions.
  • Eliminate toil; build SRE defaults into the platform.
