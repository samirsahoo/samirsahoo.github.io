GenAI Adoption Strategy for the Enterprise

How an enterprise adopts generative AI at scale: an adoption maturity model, use-case discovery and prioritisation, build vs buy vs embed, model and deployment strategy, the operating model, business case and value measurement, responsible-use guardrails, people and change, and a 24-month roadmap.

Simple definition#

A GenAI adoption strategy decides where generative AI should create value, which capabilities the organisation buys or builds, which models and deployments it standardises on, how risk is governed, and how people and processes change — so that AI moves from scattered experiments to a measured, repeatable capability.

It is the umbrella over the more specific agentic AI strategy: most enterprises adopt assistive GenAI first and agents second, on the same platform and governance.

Why adoption stalls#

Most enterprises do not fail at building a GenAI demo. They fail at the step after:

Symptom Root cause
Pilot purgatory — many pilots, few in production No shared platform, evaluation or governance path; each pilot is rebuilt from scratch
Shadow AI — staff paste data into consumer tools No approved alternative that is as easy to use
No measurable value Success defined as “users like it”, no baseline, no business owner
Data not ready Documents without owners, permissions or quality; systems without APIs
Risk blocks everything Governance designed as a one-off committee review instead of risk-tiered paved roads
Cost surprises Token spend unmetered, no chargeback, largest model used everywhere

A strategy exists to remove these causes in a deliberate order.

Adoption maturity model#

Stage What it looks like Exit criteria to move on
1 Explore Acceptable-use policy, an approved enterprise chat assistant and coding assistant, AI literacy training Shadow-AI usage measurably down; inventory of ideas
2 Experiment 3–6 funded pilots with business owners and baselines At least two pilots meet their success metric on an evaluation set
3 Formalise AI gateway, model catalogue, RAG starter, evaluation harness, risk-tiered approvals A new use case can reach production in weeks, not months
4 Scale Dozens of use cases on the platform; reusable components; cost per team visible Portfolio value reported quarterly; platform reuse > 50%
5 Transform End-to-end processes redesigned; agents with progressive autonomy; AI in products Process KPIs (cycle time, cost to serve) shifted, not just task time saved

Many large enterprises in 2026 sit between stages 3 and 4. Skipping stage 3 is the most common reason stage 4 never arrives.

The strategic pillars#

Each pillar needs an owner. The architect usually owns the platform and contributes to data and governance; the value pillar must be owned by the business.

Use-case discovery and prioritisation#

GenAI use-case archetypes#

Grouping ideas into archetypes reveals reuse: one platform capability serves many use cases.

Archetype Examples Platform capability reused
Knowledge retrieval and Q&A Policy assistant, IT/HR helpdesk, product knowledge for agents RAG with permissions
Summarisation Call notes, case histories, research digests Prompt templates, evaluation for faithfulness
Extraction and classification Invoices, KYC documents, claims, email triage Structured output, small models
Content generation Marketing drafts, RFP responses, reports Style guides, review workflows
Code and IT Coding assistants, test generation, legacy code explanation, migration Coding agents, repository context
Conversational front ends Customer chat, employee self-service Guardrails, handoff to humans
Agentic process work Case handling, reconciliation, service desk resolution Agents, tools, approvals

Scoring and the portfolio#

Score each candidate on value, feasibility and risk, with weights agreed by the business sponsors.

Weighted scoring example

Use case Value (40%) Feasibility (35%) Risk, inverted (25%) Score
IT helpdesk knowledge assistant 7 9 9 8.2
Invoice data extraction 8 8 7 7.8
Contract clause review for legal 9 6 5 7.0
Customer-facing advice chatbot 8 6 3 6.1
Automated credit decisions 9 4 1 5.3

Feasibility covers data availability and quality, integration effort and whether current models pass the task. Risk covers regulation (for example EU AI Act high-risk areas such as credit scoring), harm if wrong, and reversibility.

Build a balanced portfolio: a few quick wins to earn trust, two or three strategic bets that justify the platform, and foundations work (data, APIs) for high-value cases that are not yet feasible. Start internal and assistive, then move to customer-facing and autonomous.

From idea to scaled value#

Every prioritised use case should follow the same paved road: risk tiering sets the depth of review, a pilot on the shared platform is measured against its baseline, and only evaluation evidence moves it to go-live.

Build, buy or embed?#

Option What it is Choose when Watch out for
Buy horizontal tools Enterprise chat assistants, coding assistants, meeting assistants Broad productivity; low differentiation; fast start Data-handling terms, connector permissions, licence cost per seat vs actual use
Use AI embedded in SaaS Copilots inside CRM, ITSM, ERP, office suites The process lives in that SaaS Fragmented governance; each vendor’s AI is a separate data path to approve
Configure low-code Agent and workflow builders on a platform Departmental, moderate-risk automation Sprawl; enforce the same gateway and evaluation rules
Build on the platform Your code, your prompts, your evaluation, your integrations Differentiating processes, sensitive data, cross-system work Needs platform, skills and LLMOps maturity

Most enterprises do all four. The strategy’s job is to make sure every route uses the same identity, data-classification rules, logging and inventory — so bought and built AI can be governed as one estate.

Model strategy#

A model strategy is a portfolio, not a single vendor choice:

  • Default tiers: a mid-tier model for most work, a frontier model for hard reasoning, small models for classification, routing and guardrails, plus embedding and reranker models. See Model selection.
  • At least two providers qualified on your evaluation sets, behind an AI gateway, for resilience and negotiating power.
  • Open-weight models for restricted data, offline environments or very high volume.
  • A re-evaluation cadence: new models arrive every few months; the evaluation suite decides when to switch.
  • Fine-tuning only with evidence, after prompting and RAG baselines. See Prompting vs RAG vs fine-tuning.

Deployment strategy#

Decide deployment per data class, not once for the company: public APIs for public and internal data, cloud-managed models with private networking for most confidential data, on-premise or sovereign for restricted data, and air-gapped where required. The full decision guide, security, privacy, capacity and integration design are in On-premise and hybrid deployment.

Operating model#

  • Centralised suits stages 1–2: scarce skills, fast learning, consistent standards — but it becomes a bottleneck.
  • Hub-and-spoke suits stages 3–4: the hub runs the platform, governance and reusable components; domain teams own use cases, prompts, evaluation sets and outcomes. See Enterprise AI stack.
  • Federated suits stage 5, once paved roads and controls are mature.

Key roles

Role Responsibility
Executive sponsor Funding, priorities, removing blockers
AI product owner (per domain) Use-case outcomes, success metrics, evaluation sets, adoption
AI / GenAI engineer Prompts, retrieval, tools, agents, integration
AI platform team Gateway, model catalogue, RAG and eval tooling, LLMOps, guardrails
Data owners and stewards Content quality, classification, permissions
AI governance (risk, legal, privacy, security) Risk tiers, approvals, regulatory compliance, audits
Change and enablement Training, champions network, communications

Business case and value measurement#

Count the full cost#

Cost category Examples
Model usage Tokens, provisioned throughput, GPU hours
Platform Gateway, vector stores, observability, evaluation tooling
Licences Seats for bought assistants and SaaS copilots
Build and integration Engineering, connectors, APIs for systems of record
Quality and safety Evaluation set creation, red teaming, human review time
Run Support, on-call, model upgrades, re-evaluation
Change Training, process redesign, communications

Measure outcomes, not usage#

Level Metric Example
Adoption Active users, retention, tasks per user 70% weekly active in target group
Task Time saved, quality on evaluation set, error rate Drafting time down 40%, faithfulness ≥ 0.9
Process Cycle time, cost per case, first-contact resolution Claim handling time down 25%
Business Revenue, cost to serve, risk losses, customer satisfaction Cost to serve down 10%
Risk Incidents, policy violations, override rates No restricted-data incidents

Worked example — invoice extraction

Item Value
Volume 50,000 invoices/month
Current cost 6 minutes manual handling × €40/hour → ≈ €200,000/month
With GenAI 85% straight-through; 15% reviewed at 3 minutes → ≈ €15,000/month review
AI run cost Tokens ≈ €2,000 + platform share and support ≈ €8,000 → ≈ €10,000/month
Monthly saving ≈ €175,000, before one-off build cost

The case stands or falls on the straight-through rate at acceptable accuracy — which only the evaluation set can tell you before go-live.

Key conceptTime saved is not money saved

Ten minutes saved per employee per day is real only if the work is redesigned to use it: fewer handoffs, higher volumes, faster cycle times. Report process and business metrics, not just hours.

Responsible adoption and guardrails#

  • Acceptable-use policy: what data may go into which tools, disclosure of AI-generated content, prohibited uses.
  • Approved tools catalogue: the fastest way to reduce shadow AI is a sanctioned tool that is as easy as the consumer one.
  • Risk tiers: lightweight approval for low-risk internal assistants; full assessment for customer-facing, high-risk or autonomous use cases. See AI governance.
  • AI inventory: every use case, model, data class and owner registered — a prerequisite for EU AI Act, ISO/IEC 42001 and internal audit.
  • Security by default: gateway, guardrails and AI security controls built into the platform so teams inherit them.

People and change#

  • AI literacy for everyone, deeper skills for builders and reviewers. Under the EU AI Act, providers and deployers must ensure sufficient AI literacy of their staff (applicable since February 2025).
  • Champions network in each department to share prompts, patterns and wins.
  • Job and process redesign with the people who do the work; involve works councils and HR early where required.
  • Honest communication about impact on roles; adoption collapses when people suspect AI is a covert headcount programme.
  • Trust signals: show sources, make it easy to give feedback, publish quality metrics.

A 24-month roadmap#

Period Focus
0–3 months Acceptable-use policy and approved assistants; AI literacy; use-case intake and scoring; pick 3–5 pilots with owners and baselines; AI gateway in front of approved models
3–6 months Pilots on a minimal platform (gateway, RAG starter, evaluation harness, tracing); risk-tier process; model catalogue with two providers; data-classification routing rules
6–12 months First 5–10 use cases in production; chargeback; reusable components; on-prem or sovereign route for restricted data if needed; first agentic use cases with human approval
12–24 months Scale to the portfolio; hub-and-spoke operating model; process redesign in two or three value streams; agentic processes with progressive autonomy; continuous evaluation and red teaming; quarterly value reporting

Common mistakes#

Common mistakeTechnology-first strategy

Starting with “which model?” instead of “which outcomes?”. Choose use cases and metrics first; the model choice follows from evaluation.

Common mistakeBanning instead of providing

Blocking all GenAI drives staff to personal devices. Provide an approved, logged alternative and a clear policy.

Common mistakeEvery pilot builds its own stack

Separate keys, vector stores and logging per team make governance and cost control impossible. Build the platform through the first pilots.

Common mistakeMeasuring adoption as success

Usage shows interest, not value. Agree process and business metrics with a baseline before the pilot starts.

Common mistakeOne governance process for all risk levels

A full committee review for an internal summariser blocks adoption; a lightweight review for credit decisions breaks the law. Tier the process.

Review questions#

How would you structure a GenAI adoption strategy for a large enterprise? Around value and use cases, a shared platform, data readiness, risk-tiered governance, an operating model and people change — sequenced through a maturity model: explore with policy and approved tools, run measured pilots, formalise a platform and governance, scale a portfolio, then redesign processes.

How do you prioritise GenAI use cases? Group ideas into archetypes, score them on value, feasibility (data, integration, model fit) and risk with business-agreed weights, and build a balanced portfolio of quick wins, strategic bets and foundations work — starting internal and assistive.

Build, buy or embed? Buy horizontal productivity tools, use AI embedded in the SaaS where the process lives, configure low-code for departmental automation, and build on the platform for differentiating, sensitive or cross-system work — with common identity, data rules, logging and inventory across all routes.

How do you build the business case for a GenAI use case? Baseline the current process cost and quality, estimate the automated or assisted share from an evaluation set, count the full cost (tokens, platform, licences, build, review, run, change), and measure process and business outcomes, not just usage.

How do you reduce shadow AI? An approved tool that is as easy as the consumer alternative, a clear acceptable-use policy, AI literacy training, and network or DLP controls as a backstop — not a blanket ban.

Which operating model would you recommend? Centralised while skills are scarce, hub-and-spoke once a platform exists — the hub owns platform, standards and governance, domains own use cases and outcomes — and federated when paved roads and controls are mature.

Summary#

  • Adoption is a sequence: explore, experiment, formalise, scale, transform. Do not skip the platform and governance stage.
  • Prioritise by value, feasibility and risk; group use cases into archetypes to maximise reuse.
  • Buy, embed, configure and build — under one set of identity, data, logging and inventory rules.
  • Treat models as a portfolio and deployment as a per-data-class decision.
  • Move from a central CoE to hub-and-spoke as you scale.
  • Count the full cost and measure process and business outcomes.
  • Invest in literacy, champions and process redesign; adoption is a change programme.
