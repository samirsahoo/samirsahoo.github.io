A Practical Playbook for Building AI Agents: Opportunity Scoring and the A.G.E.N.T. Framework

How to find, prioritise and build successful AI agents: the sweet spot of impact, feasibility and effort, scoring impact and feasibility, the prioritisation matrix, thinking in tasks not roles, the A.G.E.N.T. framework (identity, gear and brain, execution and workflow, navigation and rules, testing and trust), a multi-agent example, implementation principles and the three horizons of agentic business models.

Simple definition#

This playbook covers the practical path from “we should use agents” to an agent that works in production: find the right opportunity, score and prioritise it, design the agent with a structured framework, and implement it with principles that avoid the most common failures.

It complements agentic AI strategy, which covers the portfolio and operating model.

Key conceptThe one idea to keep

Agents automate tasks, not roles. A person may own five processes; you may need five small, well-defined agents — and a human — to cover the same work.

Step 1 — Find the sweet spot#

Good agent opportunities sit where three things overlap:

And they are processes that agents are actually good at. Keep with people the work that depends on genuine creativity, strategic judgement, empathy or accountability that cannot be delegated.

Score impact#

Rate each criterion from 1 to 5:

Criterion 1 3 5
Time investment Under an hour per week Several hours per week Hours every day
Strategic value of freed time Little better use for it Moderate Unblocks high-value strategic work
Error-reduction potential Errors are rare Occasional significant errors Frequent or costly errors
Scalability One-off task Moderately repeatable Repeats across the organisation

Score feasibility#

Criterion 1 3 5
Process standardisation Ad hoc, no standard approach Some documentation; relies on experience Fully documented steps, decision rules and exceptions
Data and system access Data locked in legacy systems or paper Available but needs significant preparation Structured data, modern APIs

Prioritise#

Plot each candidate by impact and feasibility.

Low feasibility High feasibility
High impact Strategic projects — fix the process or data first, then automate Quick wins → start here
Low impact Avoid Opportunistic — do if cheap; good for learning

Then choose the right level of automation: deterministic, standardised, zero-error-tolerance work suits intelligent automation; variable, unstructured, judgement-heavy work suits an agentic workflow. See level 2 or level 3?

TipProve it manually first

Never automate a process that has not been done successfully by hand. Existing process documentation — steps, tools, decision trees, worked examples — is the best raw material for an agent’s instructions and evaluation set.

Step 2 — Design with the A.G.E.N.T. framework#

A.G.E.N.T. is a checklist of five design areas. Each answers one question.

Component Key question Elements Actions
A — Agent identity Who is the agent? Purpose, role, scope Write a one-sentence mission; state boundaries and responsibilities; align with the intended use
G — Gear and brain What powers it? Model, tools, knowledge sources Choose the model for quality and cost; implement the needed tools and APIs; curate high-quality knowledge
E — Execution and workflow How does it work? Inputs and outputs, workflow, triggers Standardise input/output formats; define the workflow; set triggers (schedule, event, request)
N — Navigation and rules How does it decide? Processing rules, safety mechanisms, transparency Filtering and prioritisation rules; rate limits, circuit breakers, human escalation; decision logs
T — Testing and trust How do we improve and scale it? Real-world testing, feedback, monitoring, scalability Simulate real cases and edge cases; collect feedback; monitor logs; plan for scale

How it maps to the rest of this handbook:

A.G.E.N.T. Read more
Agent identity What is an agent?, Agent identity and access
Gear and brain Model selection, Tool calling, MCP, RAG
Execution and workflow Workflow patterns, Planning
Navigation and rules Reliability and guardrails, AI security
Testing and trust Agent evaluation, User feedback

Example: a newsletter team of agents#

A daily industry newsletter is a classic first multi-agent project: clear steps, low risk, measurable output.

Each agent has one job and few tools; the human editor stays in the loop at the point of judgement. See multi-agent systems.

Step 3 — Implementation principles#

Grouped by phase, these are the lessons that most often separate working agents from abandoned pilots.

Defining the agent#

  • Precise instructions win. Invest in the purpose, role and scope. Examples teach more than descriptions; put the most important instructions where the model weighs them most (often at the end).
  • The simpler, the better. Every extra agent, tool or task adds cost and maintenance. Start minimal and grow.
  • Few tools per agent. A narrowly scoped agent with one or two tools is more reliable than a multi-purpose one.
  • Divide and conquer. Build one component at a time instead of the whole system at once.

Designing for success#

  • Design for collaboration with people. Augment rather than replace; keep humans in the loop for quality and strategic decisions.
  • Integrate where users already work — the chat tool, CRM or ticketing system they use. An inconvenient agent is unused.
  • Let agents check their own results — give them tools to verify that an action succeeded.
  • Standardise inputs and outputs with strict schemas to avoid errors from mismatched data.
  • Separate knowledge from actions. Keep what the agent knows apart from what it can do — better for security and maintenance.

Implementing and operating#

  • Speed over perfection in platform choice: start with something workable and iterate.
  • Design for failure: error handling, circuit breakers, graceful degradation and human escalation. Agents will fail; recovery is what matters.
  • Build decision trails: log the reasoning and evidence behind every decision for accountability and improvement.
  • Collect continuous feedback from users and system metrics.
  • Use progressive trust: start with heavy oversight and reduce it as the agent proves reliable — see rolling out safely.
  • Test with real-world scenarios, including edge cases and unexpected inputs.
  • Expect iteration: plan several refinement cycles.
  • Deployment is harder than building: integration, security review and change management often exceed development effort — budget at least as much for them.
  • Start small, then scale: deliver value with the smallest useful component, then expand.

Step 4 — Business models: three horizons#

Beyond internal efficiency, agents create new ways to earn money. A three-horizon view helps sequence ambition:

Horizon Focus Examples
1 — Optimise Use agents to make current operations cheaper and faster Support automation, document processing, internal assistants
2 — Extend Offer agent-powered services alongside existing products Agent-driven advisory, outcome-based services, agents sold to customers
3 — Transform New business models built around agents Agent marketplaces, agent-to-agent commerce, fully agent-operated services priced per outcome

Most organisations should fund horizon 1 for returns and learning, run a few horizon-2 experiments, and watch horizon 3. Pricing shifts too — from per seat to per task or per outcome — which changes how you measure value. See measuring value and cost.

Common mistakes#

Common mistakeAutomating a role

Scoping an agent as “a virtual analyst”. Break the role into tasks; automate the tasks that score well; keep the rest with people.

Common mistakeAutomating a broken process

Agents amplify whatever process they run. Standardise and document it first — low feasibility scores usually mean process work, not AI work.

Common mistakeUnder-budgeting deployment

Treating a working prototype as nearly done. Integration, security, approvals and adoption are most of the effort.

Review questions#

How do you identify a good agent opportunity? Look for the overlap of high business impact, feasibility with current technology and data, and reasonable effort. Score impact (time spent, value of freed time, error reduction, scalability) and feasibility (process standardisation, data and system access), then prioritise high-impact, high-feasibility quick wins.

What does “think tasks, not roles” mean? Agents are reliable on specific, well-defined tasks, not on whole jobs. Decompose roles into tasks and automate those that fit, often with several small agents and a human.

Explain the A.G.E.N.T. framework. Agent identity (purpose, role, scope), Gear and brain (model, tools, knowledge), Execution and workflow (inputs/outputs, workflow, triggers), Navigation and rules (decision rules, safety mechanisms, transparency) and Testing and trust (real-world testing, feedback, monitoring, scalability).

What is a progressive trust model? Starting an agent with strong human oversight and reducing it in stages as monitored performance proves reliability — for example, from approve-every-action to approve-above-threshold to sampled review.

Why is deployment often harder than building an agent? Integration with existing systems, identity and permissions, security review, monitoring, change management and user adoption take more time and resources than the agent logic itself.

Review tipShow the scoring

When asked which process you would automate first, score two or three candidates on impact and feasibility out loud and pick the quick win. It demonstrates structured, value-led thinking.

Summary#

  • Target the sweet spot of impact, feasibility and effort; score and plot candidates.
  • Automate tasks, not roles; prove processes manually before automating them.
  • Design with A.G.E.N.T.: identity, gear and brain, execution and workflow, navigation and rules, testing and trust.
  • Keep agents simple, integrated, verifiable and logged; design for failure and grow trust progressively.
  • Sequence business ambition across three horizons: optimise, extend, transform.
