AI Governance and Responsible AI

How enterprises govern AI: responsible-AI principles, the EU AI Act risk tiers and obligations, NIST AI RMF and its generative AI profile, ISO/IEC 42001, model risk management in financial services, GDPR, the AI governance operating model, use-case lifecycle, documentation and controls mapped to architecture.

Simple definition#

AI governance is the set of policies, roles, processes and technical controls that make sure an organisation’s AI is lawful, safe, fair, secure, transparent and accountable — and that someone is answerable for each system. Responsible AI names the principles; governance is how you put them into practice.

Why it matters#

  • Regulation: the EU AI Act, data-protection law (GDPR), sector rules (banking model-risk management, healthcare, employment) and emerging national laws.
  • Risk: wrong decisions, discrimination, data leaks, security incidents, reputational damage.
  • Scale: without an inventory and guardrails, organisations do not know where AI is used, by whom, on what data.
  • Trust: customers, regulators and employees need confidence before AI is used in important decisions.

Good governance enables AI adoption: clear rules and paved roads let teams ship faster than ad-hoc reviews.

Responsible-AI principles#

Principle In practice
Accountability A named owner for each AI system; decisions are traceable
Transparency & explainability Users know they deal with AI; decisions can be explained; documentation exists
Fairness Test for bias across groups; mitigate and monitor
Privacy Data minimisation, lawful basis, purpose limitation, retention
Safety & reliability Evaluated before release, monitored in production, fails safely
Security Protected against manipulation and misuse (AI security)
Human oversight Humans can understand, intervene in and override AI decisions
Sustainability Consider compute and energy cost in model choices

The EU AI Act#

The EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) is the first comprehensive AI law. It applies to providers and deployers of AI systems placed on or used in the EU market, regardless of where they are based.

High-risk obligations (providers) include: a risk-management system; data governance for training data; technical documentation; automatic logging; transparency and instructions for deployers; human oversight; accuracy, robustness and cybersecurity; a quality-management system; conformity assessment and registration. Deployers must use the system as instructed, ensure human oversight by competent people, monitor operation, keep logs, and in some cases perform a fundamental-rights impact assessment.

General-purpose AI (GPAI) models — the foundation models themselves — have their own obligations for providers (technical documentation, copyright policy, training-data summary), with extra duties for models with systemic risk.

AI literacy: organisations must ensure staff who operate or use AI have sufficient AI literacy.

Timeline (simplified): entered into force August 2024; prohibitions and AI literacy from February 2025; GPAI obligations from August 2025; most high-risk obligations were scheduled from August 2026 to August 2027, and the EU has proposed adjusting parts of this timeline — always check the current dates for your use case.

Penalties: up to €35 million or 7% of global annual turnover for prohibited practices; lower tiers for other breaches.

TipClassify every use case

Most enterprise generative-AI use cases — internal knowledge assistants, coding assistants, summarisation — are minimal or limited risk. Credit decisions, hiring and employee evaluation are high risk. Classification drives the controls you need.

The Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1) lists GenAI-specific risks — confabulation (hallucination), information security, data privacy, harmful content, intellectual property, value-chain and component integration, environmental impact, and others — with suggested actions for each.

ISO/IEC 42001#

ISO/IEC 42001:2023 specifies an AI management system (AIMS), certifiable like ISO 27001 for information security: policy, roles, risk and impact assessment, controls (Annex A), operation, monitoring, internal audit and continual improvement. Related standards: ISO/IEC 23894 (AI risk management) and ISO/IEC 42005 (AI system impact assessment).

Financial services: model risk management#

Banks and insurers already govern models under model risk management (MRM) — for example the US SR 11-7 guidance and European supervisory expectations. LLMs fit into MRM with adjustments:

MRM element LLM adaptation
Model inventory Include LLM-based applications, prompts and vendor models; record versions
Development documentation Purpose, design (RAG, tools), data, prompts, limitations
Independent validation Evaluation sets, robustness and bias testing, red teaming, conceptual soundness of the design
Ongoing monitoring Online evaluation, drift, incidents, vendor model changes
Third-party risk Vendor due diligence, contracts, exit plans (also required by operational-resilience rules such as DORA in the EU)

Data protection (GDPR)#

  • Lawful basis and purpose limitation for personal data sent to models or used for fine-tuning.
  • DPIA (data-protection impact assessment) for high-risk processing.
  • Automated decisions (Art. 22): individuals have rights regarding solely automated decisions with legal or similarly significant effects — human review and explanation.
  • Data subject rights — access, rectification and erasure apply to stored conversations, memories and logs.
  • International transfers — model hosting region and provider sub-processors.

Operating model#

Three lines of defence: product teams own and control risk (1st), risk/compliance set policy and challenge (2nd), internal audit assures (3rd).

Use-case lifecycle with governance gates#

Low-risk use cases get a lightweight self-assessment; high-risk ones get full review, independent validation and formal sign-off.

Documentation artefacts#

Artefact Content
AI inventory entry System, owner, purpose, users, models and versions, data categories, risk tier, status
Model / system card Intended use, limitations, evaluation results, known risks, human oversight
Impact assessment Effects on individuals and fundamental rights; DPIA where personal data is involved
Evaluation report Datasets, metrics, results, bias and robustness tests, red-team findings
Monitoring plan Metrics, thresholds, review cadence, incident procedure
AI-BOM Models, datasets, libraries, MCP servers and other components with versions and sources

Governance requirement Technical control
Know where AI is used AI gateway as the only path to models; inventory linked to gateway credentials
Approved models only Model catalogue enforced at the gateway
Data protection DLP/PII masking at gateway; region-pinned endpoints; retention policies on traces
Transparency UI disclosure that users interact with AI; labels on generated content
Human oversight Approval workflows, override capability, escalation paths
Logging and traceability Traces with model and prompt versions; immutable audit logs

Common mistakes#

Common mistakeGovernance as a one-off approval

AI behaviour changes with data, prompts and vendor model updates. Governance must include monitoring and periodic review, not just a launch sign-off.

Common mistakeThe same heavy process for every use case

Applying high-risk procedures to a meeting-summary tool drives teams to shadow AI. Make governance proportionate to risk.

Review questions#

How would you set up AI governance in a large enterprise? An AI governance council with risk, legal, privacy, security, architecture and business; policies for acceptable use and risk tiers; an AI inventory; a risk-proportionate lifecycle with gates (triage, design review, evaluation, approval, monitoring, retirement); a platform team providing paved roads with built-in controls (gateway, guardrails, evaluation tooling); and alignment with frameworks such as the EU AI Act, NIST AI RMF and ISO/IEC 42001.

Explain the EU AI Act’s risk categories. Unacceptable risk is prohibited (for example social scoring); high risk (credit, employment, critical infrastructure, education and others) carries strict obligations — risk management, data governance, documentation, logging, human oversight, accuracy and robustness, conformity assessment; limited risk carries transparency duties such as disclosing chatbots and labelling deepfakes; minimal risk has no specific obligations. General-purpose AI models have separate provider obligations.

What are the four functions of the NIST AI RMF? Govern (policies, roles, culture), Map (context and risks), Measure (assessment and metrics), Manage (prioritise, mitigate, monitor, respond).

How do LLMs fit into model risk management in a bank? Register LLM applications and vendor models in the model inventory, document design and limitations, validate independently with evaluation sets, robustness, bias and red-team testing, monitor continuously including vendor model changes, and manage third-party and concentration risk.

How does architecture support governance? A central AI gateway enforces approved models, data-protection policies, quotas and logging; tracing records model and prompt versions; evaluation gates sit in CI; approval workflows provide human oversight; kill switches and rollbacks support incident response.

Review tipConnect regulation to controls

Translate each obligation into a concrete control in the architecture — for example “human oversight → approval workflow with evidence view and override, logged”.

Summary#

  • Governance turns responsible-AI principles into owners, policies, processes and technical controls.
  • EU AI Act: prohibited, high-risk, limited-risk, minimal-risk tiers plus GPAI obligations; classify every use case.
  • NIST AI RMF (Govern, Map, Measure, Manage) and ISO/IEC 42001 structure the management system; banks extend model risk management.
  • Make governance proportionate, continuous and enforced through architecture.
